## Beyond Consent: Helping Shape the Future of Artificial Intelligence Through Indigenous Data Sovereignty

*AI is changing the world much faster than governments can regulate it. From education and healthcare to finance and law enforcement, AI and its systems are making decisions that affect millions of people every day. As AI becomes increasingly integrated into society, discussions about ethics need to be made paramount just as much as the technological side.*

*As AI grows at an exponential rate and becomes increasingly dependent on large amounts of data, important conversations arise, such as the familiar ones: algorithmic bias, privacy, transparency, and accountability. While these issues deserve the conversations they are having, they cannot overshadow other ethical challenges, and questions such as: **How should AI systems treat Indigenous knowledge and data?**  For Indigenous communities, the answers are not always clear, and many concerns are paramount, more than just individual privacy. Many factors must be considered, such as cultural identity, collective ownership, self-determination, and respect for the knowledge passed down.*

*AI depends on data! The quality, ownership, and governance of that data directly influence the ethics of AI systems. This raises another important question: **Can AI truly be ethical if it ignores Indigenous Data Sovereignty?** I believe the answer is no, and this blog will explore why Indigenous Data Sovereignty should be an essential part of AI governance and why ethical AI requires more than just following existing laws.*

## What is Indigenous Data Sovereignty?

Indigenous Data Sovereignty is the core principle that Indigenous peoples have the inherent right to govern the collection, ownership, storage, access, and use of all data relating to their communities, cultures, languages, territories, and histories and knowledge **(First Nations Information Governance Centre [FNIGC], n.d.)**.

Traditional privacy laws tend to focus on protecting individuals, while Indigenous Data Sovereignty explicitly recognizes that data can belong to an entire community. The traditional stories, sacred teachings, language, and cultural practices are more often collectively rather than individually owned and therefore cannot be treated like ordinary public information **(FNIGC, n.d.)**.

In Canada, these ideas are reflected in the OCAP® Principles, developed by the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC):

-	Ownership 
-	Control
-	Access
-	Possession

These core principles establish that First Nations should determine how information about their communities and peoples is collected, stored, shared, and used **(FNIGC, n.d.)**.

OCAP® was originally developed for research and information governance to help reframe their models away from colonial ones and towards Indigenous Data Sovereignty. Its principles are becoming increasingly relevant as AI systems rely on enormous datasets from numerous sources **(FNIGC, n.d.)**.

## Ethical AI Frameworks and the Challenges They Create

AI learns primarily by processing an excessive amount of information. It relies on data, and unlike traditional software that follows fixed code and instructions, AI systems learn by identifying patterns within these enormous collections of data. The quality, diversity, and quantity of this data directly influence how accurate AI systems can become. As organizations strive to build more competent and capable AI models, there is an increasing demand for large, diverse datasets.

Training data may include:

-	Books
-	Academic research
-	Government publications
-	Websites
-	Images
-	Videos
-	Public databases
-	Social media

Ethical challenges arise because publicly available information is not always information that communities intend to become part of commercial AI systems **(FNIGC, n.d.)**. This distinction is especially important when Indigenous knowledge and cultural information are involved.

For Indigenous communities, this extends far beyond copyright or privacy and recognizes that information about Indigenous communities is often more than just simple data. These AI systems may learn from traditional knowledge, language resources, historical documents, cultural materials, and generations of lived experience without meaningful consultation, community involvement, or permission. This knowledge and data may be collectively owned and governed by the community rather than a single individual **(FNIGC, n.d.)**.

When AI systems are trained using Indigenous data without meaningful consultation or community involvement, several ethical concerns emerge. Communities lose control over how they are represented, culturally significant information may be spun out of context, and others profit off these systems that rely on Indigenous knowledge without sharing or obtaining consent. While there are times this could be legally permissible, legality does not always equate to ethical responsibility **(Floridi, 2023)**.

These concerns raise insights into why Indigenous Data Sovereignty is more than a question of privacy. It's about respecting community autonomy, protecting cultural heritage, ensuring fairness, and maintaining accountability. As AI continues to evolve, those in charge of creating the systems must consider not only whether data can be used legally but also whether it respects the rights, values, and expectations of the communities from which it originates **(Floridi, 2023)**.


## Applying Ethical Concepts to AI

AI should be evaluated against strict ethical standards that guide how data is collected, how systems are built, and how the technology affects people and communities. Indigenous Data Sovereignty provides an excellent example of why these principles are paramount. It reminds us that AI is not only a technological marvel but also a social responsibility that requires respect for culture, community, and human rights **(Floridi, 2023)**.

### Beneficence

This is the principle of creating positive outcomes and promoting human well-being. AI has the potential to support Indigenous communities by preserving dying languages, improving access to educational resources, environmental monitoring, and improving healthcare services. These benefits should be developed in tandem with Indigenous communities rather than imposed on them. When Indigenous Peoples help shape AI systems, the technology is more likely to produce outcomes that align with the community's priorities and contribute to long-term social well-being **(Floridi, 2023)**.

### Non-Maleficence

This emphasizes the responsibility to avoid causing harm at all costs. AI systems trained on Indigenous knowledge without any meaningful consultation may misrepresent cultural traditions, reproduce sacred teachings out of context, or even reinforce stereotypes that have a history of marginalizing. Even with good intentions, using this sensitive data without community involvement can cause unintentional lasting harm **(Floridi, 2023)**.

### Autonomy

This recognizes the importance of allowing individuals and communities to make well-informed decisions about matters that directly affect them. Indigenous Data Sovereignty reflects this principle by maintaining that Indigenous communities should have authority over how their data is collected, shared, and used in AI systems. Respecting this autonomy happens when communities are engaged throughout the AI development process. Meaningful consent is obtained where necessary and appropriate, and they are ensured a genuine voice in decisions that involve their knowledge, culture, and data **(Floridi, 2023)**.

### Justice

The focus here is on fairness and the equitable distribution of AI's benefits and risks. Throughout history, Indigenous communities have often been exposed to the extraction of knowledge and resources without receiving equal benefits in return. Ethical AI should avoid repeating the mistakes of the past by ensuring Indigenous communities participate in governance, benefit from the use of their data, and are protected from all discriminatory outcomes. It also involves creating AI systems that are inclusive and equitable **(Floridi, 2023)**.

### Explicability

This combines transparency and accountability by ensuring that AI systems are easy to understand and that organizations remain responsible for their decisions. Indigenous communities should always know what information has been collected, how it will be used, and whether it has contributed to the development of any AI systems. Those building these systems need to be transparent about the origins of training data and willing to explain how these AI models generate their outputs. If an AI system misrepresents Indigenous knowledge or produces harmful results, these organizations should be fully accountable for correcting these issues and working in tandem with affected communities **(Floridi, 2023)**.

## Conclusion and a Real-World Example

AI has the potential to improve lives in many amazing ways, but its success should not be measured by how intelligent or efficient it becomes. Responsible AI must also be judged by how well it respects the people and communities whose data make it possible. These ethical concepts we explored provide a strong foundation for evaluating AI systems and encouraging developers to create technology that promotes societal well-being, avoids harm, respects community decision-making, ensures fairness, and remains transparent and accountable **(Floridi, 2023)**.

When these principles are considered alongside Indigenous Data Sovereignty, they underscore the importance of recognizing that data is not merely a resource. For many Indigenous communities, this data represents their culture, language, history, and identity **(FNIGC, n.d.)**. Respecting these connections is paramount when AI developers seek to move beyond legal compliance and actively involve Indigenous communities in the decisions about how their data is collected and shared.

A real-world example can be found in the First Nations Information Governance Centre **(FNIGC)**, and its OCAP® Principles (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession). Developed to guide First Nations research and data governance, the OCAP® Principles have become an important framework for discussions about the ethical use of Indigenous data in emerging technologies, including AI **(FNIGC, n.d.)**. For example, as AI applications are researched and explored for Indigenous language revitalization and digital preservation, questions arise about who controls the data used to train these systems, who benefits from this technology, and how this culturally significant knowledge will be properly protected **(Pinhanez et al., 2024)**. The OCAP® Principles provide guidance for addressing all these questions and issues by emphasizing community ownership, control, access, and possession of data. This ensures respect for Indigenous rights and supports collaboration rather than extraction **(FNIGC, n.d.)**.
