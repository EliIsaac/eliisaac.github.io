# Theoretical Scenario

At the completion of a course, participants are given a survey of 10 questions. To perform the survey, participants access a URL in their web browser that is made available for a short period of time and requires that students log in with their username and password for the organization. 

The beginning of the survey prompts for the name of the course, but not their name. Instead, the survey description on this page indicates that all answers and the survey itself will remain anonymous. 

Executive management wishes to use the results of the survey to determine which courses require redesign or updating. You are the data analyst assigned to this task and are required to provide actionable insights for this purpose to executive management.

## 1.	 Based on the information provided, what ethical issues are apparent in the design of this survey and in the sample question?
 
a. False Anonymity

Participants needed to use their credentials to log in, yet the survey claimed to be anonymous.

-	Would the system truly be anonymous?
-	Where is the login information stored?
-	Would it be possible to link responses back to individuals?

This could be very misleading and violates participant trust.

b. Lack of Transparency

The participants are never told:

-	What data is being collected.
-	Who has access to it.
-	The length of time it will be stored.
-	If their login information is retained.

Participants can’t provide proper informed consent if this information isn't presented.

c. Potential for Response Bias

Students may fear negative outcomes if they criticize a course.

This could cause:

-	Inflated ratings
-	Untruthful responses
-	Lack of clarity and usefulness in the data

d. Design Bias in the Survey

The questions could be worded in a way that responses could unintentionally steer participants towards certain answers.

Using poorly designed questions like this can lead to misleading data collection.

## 2.	 What other ethical issues could be present for this survey? 

a. Data Security

-	How is the data from the survey stored?
-	Is it properly encrypted?
-	Who has access to the data?
-	What data is being recorded?

If security is weak and more data than needed is collected, personal information could easily be exposed.

b. Function Creep

The data that is collected could potentially be used to:

-	Instructor evaluations
-	Track student performance
-	Review employee performance

Using data beyond its original intent without consent is unethical.

c. Re-Identification Risk

Even without names, combinations of information could identify the participants. 

-	Course name
-	Submission time
-	Class size
-	Demographic information
    
It can be difficult to guarantee true anonymity.

d. Lack of Data Retention Policies

-	No indication of how long the data will be stored
-	No indication of when it will be deleted
-	No indication of who will be responsible for deleting it

## 3. What recommendations would you make to executive management?

a. Be Truthful About Anonymity

Do not claim that the survey is anonymous unless it is technically impossible to identify the participants.

b. Improve Transparency

Provide a clear privacy statement that explains:

-	What data is collected
-	Why this data is collected
-	Who will have access to it
-	How long it will be stored

c. Separate Authentication from Responses

Login only verifies eligibility; once verified, the user's identity is immediately decoupled from their answers.

d. Limit Access

A very limited number of authorized personnel should have access to the survey's raw data.

e. Establish Retention Policies

Define:

-	How long is data stored?	
-	When will it be deleted?
-	Procedures for secure deletion

f. Report Aggregated Data Results

Provide summaries instead of individual responses.

For example:

92% of the students felt that the course material was current and up to date.

This is better than exposing individual answers.

## 4.	If executive management rejects your recommendations and insists there are no issues, how could you proceed ethically?

a.	Document Your Recommendations and Concerns

Keep written records of:

-	Identified risks
-	Recommendations provided
-	Concerns and your reasons for them

This demonstrates professional due diligence.

b.	Escalate Concerns if Necessary

Speak with:

-	Privacy officers
-	Ethics committees
-	Data governance teams
-	Legal departments 

Follow organizational policies

c.	Advocate for Participants

Continue educating decision-makers on:

-	Privacy risks
-	Legal obligations
-	Ethical responsibilities

d.	Follow a Professional Code of Ethics

As a data analyst, it is your responsibility to think beyond management requests.

Your duties include:

-	Protecting participant privacy
-	Maintaining honesty
-	Preventing misuse of data
-	Acting in the public interest
	
## Conclusion

In conclusion, this scenario has demonstrated how ethical concerns in data science often extend beyond the simple collection and analysis of the data. Although the survey was designed to improve the course and provide management with clear, actionable information, several ethical issues became apparent when the way the data was collected and used was examined. The most egregious concern is the contradiction between requiring participants to log in with their organizational credentials while simultaneously claiming the survey is anonymous. This is a clear disrespect of Spectrum Balance and can undermine trust and create uncertainty about the protection of the participants.

Other important ethical considerations include informed consent, transparency, data security, and the potential misuse of information.  The participants have the right to understand what information is being collected, how it will be used, who has access to it, and how long it will be stored. If there is no clear communication, participants may feel pressured to provide dishonest responses, undermining the ethical integrity and usefulness of the collected data. Additionally, organizations must be aware of re-identification and function creep when data is collected for one purpose and later used for another without the consent or knowledge of those who provided it.

As a professional data analyst, responsibility extends beyond simply providing your technical expertise. Data professionals have an ethical and moral obligation to advocate for fairness, privacy, and accountability throughout the data lifecycle. If your employers choose to ignore your legitimate concerns, it is still important to document recommendations, escalate concerns through appropriate channels, and avoid participating in misleading practices. Ultimately, ethical data science is not only about compliance with rules and regulations: it’s about building trust and ensuring the responsible use of data to benefit organizations and individuals equally without causing harm.
